I WAS THERE - Episode 1: "The Night the Titanic Sank"

---

My name is Ava. I have seen things that happened long before you were born.
And tonight, I'm going to tell you about the coldest night of my life.
April fourteenth, 1912. I was on the Titanic.

I was nineteen. I had a third-class ticket, one small suitcase, and a letter from my aunt in New York. She had found me a job. A new life. All I had to do was cross the ocean.

And what a ship to cross it on. The Titanic was the largest moving object people had ever built. Longer than four city blocks. Ten decks high. Electric lights, a swimming pool, a gym. Even in third class, we had clean sheets and hot food. For a girl like me, it felt like a palace.

We left Southampton on April tenth. Four days later, we were in the middle of the North Atlantic.

That Sunday, the air turned sharp. By evening it was freezing. The sea was perfectly flat. Like black glass. No wind. No moon. Just more stars than I had ever seen.

What I didn't know was that all day long, other ships had been sending warnings by radio. Ice. Lots of ice. Right ahead of us.

At 11:40 at night, I was already in my bunk.
I felt it before I heard it. A long, low shudder. Like the whole ship had scraped against a wall.

Up in the crow's nest, the lookouts had seen the iceberg too late. The officers turned the ship, but the ice cut along her side, below the waterline. Water started pouring into the front compartments.

At first, nobody was scared. Some passengers played with pieces of ice that had fallen on the deck. People said the Titanic couldn't sink.

But the man who designed her, Thomas Andrews, walked through the ship and did the math. He told the captain she had maybe two hours left.

Just after midnight, the order came. Put on your lifebelts. Get to the boats.

Here is the part that still makes me angry. The Titanic had twenty lifeboats. Enough for about one thousand one hundred and seventy-eight people. There were more than two thousand two hundred people on board.

The rule was women and children first. But in the dark and the confusion, many boats left half empty. Some people simply didn't believe it was real. It was warmer inside. Why get into a tiny boat on a freezing ocean?

For people in third class, getting to the deck was a maze. Narrow corridors. Endless stairs. Gates that kept third class apart from everyone else. I got lost twice. A steward I had never met took my hand and led me up, deck after deck. I never learned his name.

When I finally reached the boat deck, it was after one in the morning. The ship was tilting. The front was already under water. Rockets were flying into the sky, white stars, exploding over us. Calling for help.

And the band was still playing. Music, on a sinking ship. To keep people calm.

At five past two, I climbed into the last lifeboat lowered from the ship. Collapsible D. A crew member shouted: "Women and children only!" We dropped down the side of the ship. I watched the lights of the Titanic from the water, one row of windows at a time disappearing into the sea.

At 2:18, the lights went out.
At 2:20, she was gone.

And then came the sound I will never forget. Hundreds of voices in the dark. People in the water, calling for help. The water was twenty-eight degrees Fahrenheit. Below freezing.

Most of the boats did not go back. People were afraid they would be pulled under. Only a couple of boats went back to look for survivors.

Little by little, the voices stopped.

We sat in that boat for hours. Wet. Freezing. Holding each other. Nobody spoke.

Then, a little after three thirty, someone saw a light on the horizon. Rockets. A ship.

The Carpathia had heard the distress call and raced through the ice in the dark to reach us. At ten past four, the first lifeboat was picked up. The rescue went on until morning. They gave us blankets, coffee, and a place to cry.

About seven hundred people were saved. About one thousand five hundred were not.

Three days later, the Carpathia arrived in New York, in the rain. Thousands of people were waiting at the pier. My aunt was there. She held me so tight I could barely breathe.

---

After that night, the world changed the rules. Every ship had to carry enough lifeboats for every person on board. Radio rooms had to work day and night. And ships began patrolling the North Atlantic, watching for ice.

So that no one would ever have to hear what I heard that night.

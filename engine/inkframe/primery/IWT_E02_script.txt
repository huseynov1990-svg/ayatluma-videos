My name is Ava. I have seen things that happened long before you were born.
And today, I'm going to show you how the impossible was built.
Egypt. Four and a half thousand years ago. I was there when they built the Great Pyramid.

I was nineteen. I worked in a bakery at the edge of the desert, in a town that did not exist before the pyramid. Every morning, before the sun came up, we baked bread for the workers. Thousands of loaves. Every single day.
We baked them in heavy clay pots, shaped like bells, buried in hot ashes. My hands were always burned, and my hair always smelled of smoke.

The king was Khufu. And he wanted a tomb that would last forever.

From our town, I could see it growing. A mountain of stone, rising out of the sand, one layer at a time.

People will tell you slaves built it. That is not what I saw. The men I fed were workers. They were paid in bread, in beer, in onions and fish. Some were skilled builders who worked all year. Others were farmers who came for a few months, when the Nile flooded their fields and there was nothing to plant.

They worked in teams. And the teams had names. Proud names. One gang wrote on the stones: Friends of Khufu. Their names are still there today, painted inside the pyramid, where no one was ever supposed to see them.

Every block had to be cut, moved and lifted. More than two million of them. Most weighed more than two tons. That's heavier than a car.

How do you move a stone like that without wheels, without iron, without machines?

One morning, I didn't stay in the bakery.
My cousin worked on the river boats, and he took me with him.

The white stone for the outside of the pyramid didn't come from Giza. It came from Tura, across the Nile. Fine, bright limestone. The boats carried it down the river and into canals that ran almost to the foot of the pyramid.

The man in charge of our boat was an inspector named Merer. He had about forty men. And he kept a diary. Every day, he wrote down where they sailed, where they slept, how many stones they carried.

Four thousand five hundred years later, pieces of that diary were found in a dry harbor by the Red Sea. It is the oldest written papyrus ever found in Egypt. And it tells exactly what I saw: boats, canals, and stone, day after day.

At the harbor, the blocks were loaded onto wooden sledges. Then the men pulled. Twenty men, sometimes more, on long ropes. In front of the sledge, a boy poured water on the sand.

I laughed at first. Why would anyone water the sand?
But it worked. Wet sand is firm. The sledge slid over it much more easily. Scientists tested this thousands of years later, and they found the same thing. Pouring water can cut the force you need almost in half.

Then came the ramps. Long slopes of mud brick and rubble, climbing the side of the pyramid. Nobody today knows exactly what shape they were. But I watched the stones go up them, one by one, from sunrise to sunset.

The heavy granite for the king's burial chamber came from much farther away. From Aswan. Hundreds of kilometers up the Nile. Some of those blocks weighed as much as eighty tons.

And the work was dangerous. I saw men carried back to town with broken arms and broken legs. And I saw something I didn't expect. Doctors. Bones were set. Wounds were cleaned. Some of the workers who were hurt lived for years after. Their skeletons, found near the pyramid, show it.

When workers died, they were buried right there, beside the king's mountain. In their own small tombs. With bread and beer for the afterlife. Slaves would never have been given that honor.

The man who designed it all was the king's own relative, Hemiunu. People say he checked the stars to find true north. The sides of the pyramid point almost perfectly north, south, east and west. Even today, engineers are amazed by how close they got.

It took about twenty years.
Twenty years of bread, of ropes, of songs on the river. Babies were born in our town and grew up never knowing a world without the pyramid.

When it was finished, the pyramid was covered in polished white limestone. In the sun, it shone so bright you could hardly look at it. It stood about one hundred and forty-six meters tall.

For almost four thousand years, it was the tallest thing humans had ever built.

The white casing is mostly gone now. People took the stones to build other cities. But the pyramid is still standing. The last of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that you can still visit.

And somewhere inside, on a block no tourist will ever see, the Friends of Khufu still sign their name.

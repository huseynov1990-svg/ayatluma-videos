My name is Ava. I have seen things that happened long before you were born.
And today, I'm going to take you to a city that had one day left.
Italy. The year seventy-nine. I was there when Vesuvius woke up and buried Pompeii.

I was nineteen. I sold bread from a little counter on a busy street, near the bakery of a man named Modestus. Every morning I stacked round loaves on the counter, each one cut into eight pieces, like a wheel.

Pompeii was loud and bright and full of life. Carts rattled over the stone streets. People crossed on big stepping stones so their sandals stayed out of the mud. Fountains on every corner. Painted walls. And words everywhere. People wrote on the walls like you write on the internet today. Who was running for election. Who loved whom. Who owed money.

And above it all stood the mountain. Green slopes. Vineyards. Nobody called it a volcano. Nobody knew what it was.

Seventeen years before, a great earthquake had shaken the city. Temples cracked. Houses fell. And even now, workers were still repairing them.
In the last days, the ground trembled again. Small shakes. The wells went strange. But in Pompeii, small earthquakes were normal. So we did what everyone does. We kept working.

Then, a little after midday, the mountain exploded.

I heard it before I understood it. A deep roar, like the sky was tearing. And then I saw the cloud.
It rose straight up, higher than anything I had ever seen, and then it spread out at the top. Like a giant pine tree made of smoke.

Across the bay, a seventeen-year-old boy named Pliny was watching the same cloud. Years later, he wrote down everything he saw. His letters still exist. That is how we know what happened that day.

Then the sky started to fall.

It wasn't rain. It was stone. Small, light, grey stones, full of holes. Pumice. It rattled on the roof tiles. It bounced in the street. It piled up in the doorways.

By the afternoon, the sun was gone. It was dark like night, but there was no night. Only the sound of stones falling, and people shouting names.

Many people ran. They took what they could carry. Coins, a lamp, a little statue of their household gods. They covered their heads with pillows tied on with cloth. I saw a man carrying his old mother on his back.

Others stayed. They thought the walls would protect them. They thought it would stop.

It didn't stop. The stones fell all afternoon, all evening, all night. Roofs began to break under the weight.

Across the bay, Pliny's uncle was the admiral of the Roman fleet. His name was Pliny the Elder. When he saw the cloud, he got a message from a woman named Rectina, begging for help. So he took his warships and sailed straight toward the mountain. Not away from it. Toward it.
He never came home. But because of him, and because of his nephew's letters, the world remembers.

I left the city before dawn. I followed the road to the sea with a lamp that kept going out. My eyes burned. Every breath tasted like dust.

Behind me, in the dark, the mountain did something worse.

The cloud collapsed. A wave of burning gas and ash came rushing down the slopes, faster than any horse. It reached the city in minutes.

The people who had stayed did not have time.

Pompeii disappeared. Buried under ash and stone, deeper than a house is tall. Nearby, the town of Herculaneum was buried even deeper.

And then the world forgot it was there. For more than sixteen hundred years.

When people began digging in the seventeen hundreds, they found the city exactly as it was that day. Bread in the ovens. Eighty-one loaves still inside the bakery of Modestus, turned to charcoal. Dishes on the tables. Words on the walls.

They also found empty spaces in the hardened ash. In the eighteen sixties, an archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli had an idea. He poured plaster into those spaces. And the people of Pompeii came back. Not as bones. As shapes. A mother holding her child. A dog. A man covering his face.
Archaeologists have found the remains of more than a thousand people in the city. Most people did escape. But not everyone.

For centuries, everyone said it happened on the twenty-fourth of August. Then, in twenty eighteen, archaeologists found a few words written in charcoal on a wall, from the last weeks of the city. A date in the middle of October. Now many historians believe the eruption came in autumn. Maybe the twenty-fourth of October.

Today, millions of people walk through Pompeii every year. They cross the same stepping stones. They read the same walls.
And Vesuvius is still there. Still active. Still watching over the bay.

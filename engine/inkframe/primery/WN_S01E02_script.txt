At 1:55 in the morning, Eli Brooks opened a case he was told never to open.
And inside, he found his own face.

Last night, a stranger called Eli by mistake. Five thousand dollars to carry a metal case from locker fourteen to a blue door on Pier Nine. Don't open it. Don't call back. A man in a long grey coat followed him through the station. And now the case was ticking.

Pier Nine was dark. Rain on the water. One lamp over the blue door.

Eli knelt behind a stack of crates and put the case on the ground. Two metal latches. No lock.

He told himself he just needed to know. If it was a bomb, he would run. If it was money, he would walk away.

Click. Click.

The lid came up slowly.

There was no bomb. The ticking came from an old pocket watch, gold, scratched, lying on black foam. Its hands had stopped at 2:00.
But the ticking didn't stop.

Under the watch there was a brass key with a tag. Number nine.
And under the key, a photograph.

Eli picked it up. His hands were shaking.

It was him. Sitting in the diner on Fifth Street. Cold coffee. The letter in his hand. Tonight. Maybe one hour ago.

Somebody had taken this picture before the phone ever rang.

It wasn't the wrong number.
They had called exactly the person they wanted.

Eli closed the case. His heart was hammering so loud he almost didn't hear the footsteps.

Behind him, on the wet boards, someone was walking. Slowly. Not hiding.

The man in grey.

He stopped ten steps away. Rain dripping from his hat. For the first time, he spoke.
"Don't knock on that door, son."

His voice was old and tired.

"Who are you?" Eli said.
"Someone who made the same delivery twenty-two years ago," the man said. "And never came home."

Then the blue door opened.

Yellow light poured onto the pier. A woman stood in the doorway. Red coat. Black gloves. She was smiling like she'd been waiting for an old friend.

"Eli Brooks," she said. "You're five minutes early. I like that."

She knew his name.

"The five thousand is on the table," she said. "Bring me the case. Bring me the key. And go home, Eli. Your rent is already paid. I took care of it this morning."

Eli felt cold all over. Nobody knew about the rent. Nobody.

The man in grey didn't move. "She's not paying you for the case," he said quietly. "She's paying you for what the key opens."

The woman's smile didn't change.
"He lies, Eli. He's been lying for twenty-two years. Ask him why he's still afraid of a little gold watch."

The watch in the case ticked louder.

Eli looked at the woman. Then at the man. Then down at his own hand.
He was still holding the key. Number nine.

And then his phone buzzed.
Same unknown number. One new message.

"Whatever she says, don't give her the key. It opens the door to your father."

Eli read it twice.
His father had disappeared when Eli was two years old.
Twenty-two years ago. On a rainy night. Near the river.
Eli looked at the man in grey. At the tired eyes under the hat.
And for one second, he couldn't breathe.

The woman held out her gloved hand.
"Two o'clock, Eli," she said. "Time's up."
